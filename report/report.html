Big Data Analysis · HMDA 2024

Mortgage Denial Models

A study of how the debt-to-income (DTI) ratio and the combined loan-to-value (CLTV) ratio relate to mortgage denial. We fit logistic regression models in scikit-learn and in Spark MLlib.

Findings

  • Denial depends on sharp DTI limits, and the limit is different for each loan program. Conventional loans go from 5.4% denied at DTI 43–49 to 65% denied at DTI 50–60. FHA and VA loans stay low at DTI 50–60 (12% and 7.5%). They increase above DTI 60 (98% and 40%).
  • CLTV has a smaller effect. Only values above 100% cause a large increase. The adjusted rate goes from 6.9% to 15.7% for Conventional loans and from 7.9% to 27% for VA loans. Below 100%, the rate changes by a few points.
  • One shared DTI effect is not sufficient. Model A has main effects only and gives a ROC-AUC of 0.735. Model C adds effects for each program and gives 0.763. Model G (gradient boosting) gives 0.795. Model G is better than Model C by 0.046 PR-AUC. Thus the logistic features do not show all of the structure in the data.
  • The two software tools give the same predictions. For Models A, B and C, the test ROC-AUC differs by less than 1e-6. The coefficients of Models B and C differ, because their design is collinear. Use the adjusted rates to read these two models.

1. Question and data

The 2024 Home Mortgage Disclosure Act (HMDA) national loan-level file has 12.3 million rows. We ask one question. For home-purchase, first-lien, site-built loans on a principal residence, how do DTI and CLTV relate to denial? We also ask if the relation is different for each loan program. The models explain the data and give a benchmark. They are not an underwriting tool.

The final set has 3,208,974 rows. The overall denial rate is 8.83%. The rate for each program is: Conventional 7.70%, FHA 12.53%, VA 8.17% and USDA 12.20%. A denial is a record with action_taken = 3. Originated applications count as not denied. Approved applications that the applicant did not accept also count as not denied.

2. Method

Pipeline

Both tools read the same split and the same feature definitions. They join the data on a stored row_id. One script calculates all metrics from the saved test scores. Thus no tool grades its own result.

The model

Let \(y_i = 1\) if the lender denies application \(i\). Logistic regression gives the probability of denial as

\[ \Pr(y_i = 1 \mid x_i) \;=\; \sigma(\beta_0 + x_i^\top \beta) \;=\; \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(\beta_0 + x_i^\top \beta)}} . \]

Each \(x_i\) is a vector of 0/1 dummy columns and two standardized numbers. Both tools use the same columns in the same order. The predictors are loan type, DTI bin, CLTV bin, log income, an income-missing flag and log loan amount. A missing DTI or CLTV value has its own bin. We do not impute missing values. Race, ethnicity, sex and age are not inputs.

Both tools minimize the same objective. It has a small ridge penalty:

\[ \hat\beta \;=\; \arg\min_{\beta_0,\beta}\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \Big[-y_i \log p_i - (1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)\Big] \;+\; \frac{\lambda}{2}\,\lVert\beta\rVert_2^2, \qquad \lambda = 10^{-6}. \]

The intercept has no penalty. scikit-learn uses \(C = 1/(\lambda n)\). Spark uses regParam \(=\lambda\). Thus the two objectives are the same. We use no class weights. Thus you can interpret the coefficients, and the probabilities stay calibrated.

The model ladder

In Model C, the log-odds of denial is the sum of several terms. The terms are a loan-type shift \(\gamma_\ell\), a DTI-bin shift \(\delta_d\), a CLTV-bin shift \(\kappa_c\) and a joint-cell shift \(\theta_{dc}\). Two more terms, \(\phi_{\ell d}\) and \(\psi_{\ell c}\), give the program-specific shifts. The model also has terms for the controls. We pool the cells that have fewer than 500 train rows into one column, other_rare.

Adjusted denial rates

We want to compare programs at each DTI level and remove the effect of the other inputs. For this, we use standardization. Take the loans \(S_\ell\) of program \(\ell\). Set the DTI bin of each loan to \(d\). Keep the other features, predict, and calculate the average:

\[ \hat A_\ell(d) \;=\; \frac{1}{|S_\ell|} \sum_{i \in S_\ell} \hat p\big(x_i \text{ with DTI bin set to } d\big). \]

We use the same method for the CLTV bins. The results show associations. They do not show causes.

3. Prediction quality

We calculate the metrics on the 20% test split. ROC-AUC is the probability that a random denied loan has a higher score than a random approved loan. PR-AUC is the average precision. It gives more weight to the rare denied class. A model with no skill has a PR-AUC that is equal to the denial rate, 0.088.

Log loss and the Brier score show the quality of the probabilities. The Brier score is \(\frac1n\sum_i (p_i - y_i)^2\). The intervals are 95% bootstrap intervals from 200 resamples.

Calibration

4. Effect of DTI and CLTV

Model A: odds ratios

An odds ratio of 1 means no change. A DTI above 60 multiplies the odds of denial by 89. A DTI of 50–60 multiplies them by 4.2. These values are against a DTI below 36. A CLTV above 100 multiplies the odds by 2.31.

After the controls, the odds for a VA loan are 0.36 times the odds for a Conventional loan. The odds for an application with no income are 3.9 times higher. Thus a missing value carries information.

The odds ratio is \(\mathrm{OR}_j = e^{\beta_j}\). The 95% interval is \(\exp(\hat\beta_j \pm 1.96\,\mathrm{se}_j)\). We calculate the variance as \(\mathrm{Var}(\hat\beta) \approx (X^\top W X + n\lambda I_0)^{-1}\), where \(W = \mathrm{diag}\big(\hat p_i(1-\hat p_i)\big)\). Model A uses one DTI effect for all programs. This is too limited, as the next figures show.

Adjusted denial rate by DTI (Model C)

Conventional loans have a denial rate of approximately 5% up to DTI 49. In the 50–60 bin, the rate is 65%. FHA and VA loans have low rates at DTI 50–60 (12% and 7.5%). Above DTI 60, the rates are 98% and 40%. The USDA rate increases earlier, at DTI 43–49 (9% to 24%).

The data has no lender rules and no credit scores. Thus the model cannot show the reason for these steps. HMDA gives DTI as integers only from 36 to 49. All other values are in wide bins. Thus we cannot find the limits more accurately.

Model A passes all checks. For Models B and C, all checks on the predictions pass. Only the coefficient check does not pass. The largest differences are 0.16 and 0.19, and the tolerance is 1e-3.

Cause of the coefficient difference in Models B and C.

Models B and C have the DTI main effects, the CLTV main effects and the full DTI×CLTV cross. These columns are collinear. For each DTI bin \(d\), the sum of the cells in that bin is equal to the indicator of that bin:

\[ \sum_{c}\mathbf 1[\text{DTI}=d,\ \text{CLTV}=c] \;=\; \mathbf 1[\text{DTI}=d]. \]

Thus many sets of coefficients give the same predictions. Only the ridge penalty of \(10^{-6}\) selects one set. The two tools stop at different points along a flat direction. The differences are equal and opposite between a main effect and its cell dummy.

Spark also stopped at its limit of 200 iterations for Models C, D, E and F. Thus it did not reach the tolerance of \(10^{-7}\). A higher limit can decrease the gap for Model C. We did not test this.

We did not change the tolerances or the results. To read Models B and C, use the adjusted rates. They do not depend on how the tool divides the redundant weight.

The fit times for Model A were 6 s (scikit-learn) and 15 s (Spark). For Model C they were 60 s and 89 s. Each value is from one run on a laptop. Spark cached its input. These values are not a benchmark.

6. Limits

  • Associations, not causes. The data has no credit score, assets, employment history or lender rules.
  • No application date. We split the data at random, with strata of denial and loan type. The split is not by time.
  • Missing DTI and CLTV values are not random. Conventional loans with a missing DTI have a denial rate of approximately 15%. FHA loans have approximately 34%. Each missing value has its own bin.
  • DTI is coarse. HMDA gives integers only from 36 to 49.
  • USDA has few loans. Each of its two highest DTI bins has approximately 425 loans. Treat these estimates as weak.
  • The models do not use protected attributes. This report does not test for disparate impact.
  • We inferred the cohort filters. The filters for principal residence and 1–4 units give the same counts as the proposal. The proposal text does not state them.
  • We did not do two optional items. These are the fit-time benchmark and the Spark fits for each program alone.
